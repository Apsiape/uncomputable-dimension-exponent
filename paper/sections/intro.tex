\section{One fixed game}\label{sec:intro}
For a finite nonlocal game, near-perfect play can require arbitrarily large local dimension. We ask whether the exponent of that growth can be uncomputable for one fixed game. This places the uncomputable real inside a single finite specification: the input to the asymptotic problem is only the desired accuracy. Undecidability of a decision problem in which the game varies does not answer this question.

Fix a standard effective enumeration of programs and let $K\subseteq\N=\{0,1,\ldots\}$ be its halting set. All logarithms without a displayed base are base two. For a game $G$ of quantum value one, let $D_G(\eps)$ be the least integer $d$ for which a strategy on $\C^d\otimes\C^d$ wins with probability at least $1-\eps$. Smaller local spaces may be padded.
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
There is a fixed finite two-player Boolean constraint-system game $G$, with rational question probabilities, such that $\omega_q(G)=1$, no finite-dimensional strategy wins perfectly, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main}
 D_G(\eps)=\eps^{-(3+\thetaK)/4+o(1)},\qquad
 \thetaK=\sum_{e\in K}4^{-(e+1)}.
\end{equation}
The exponent is Turing-equivalent to $K$. The sequence $\log D_G(2^{-n})/n$ has no computable certified rate of convergence.
\end{theorem}

The theorem concerns local dimension, not distillable entanglement. Its game is an ordinary Boolean constraint-system (BCS) game, rather than a linear system game. The computable baseline of the exponent is $3/4$. These distinctions delimit what is proved; the corresponding distillation rate, a linear-system realization with this rate, and a smaller baseline remain open.

The existence of computable increasing rational sequences with uncomputable limits is classical, beginning with Specker~\cite{Spe49}. The issue here is their realization as the dimension law of a finite game. The separated base-four encoding of $K$ is used only to make its Turing degree explicit.

There are three main points. First, a finite set of bounded polynomial relations can be compiled into Boolean constraints without changing the asymptotic precision scale. Second, a finite program can be forced to start at every finite fuel scale, while finite models still close with exponentially small defects. Third, a dimension count on the actual density matrix converts a non-effective flag-density limit into a matched dimension exponent.

\subsection{Related work and credit}
Mousavi, Nezhadi and Yuen prove that deciding whether the quantum value is exactly one is $\Pi^0_2$-complete~\cite{MNY20,MNY22}. In particular, there is no total computable dimension bound, uniform over all value-one games and all precisions. Indeed, such a bound would make value one a universal integer quantification of decidable bounded-dimensional feasibility statements, placing that problem in $\Pi^0_1$. Their obstruction is uniform over games. Theorem~\ref{thm:main} instead identifies a convergent exponent for one game. The theorem $\mathrm{MIP}^*=\mathrm{RE}$ puts halting information in the value of a computable family of games~\cite{JNVWY}; it does not by itself identify a resource exponent of a fixed value-one game.

The algebraic connection between constraint games and operator assignments goes back to Cleve and Mittal~\cite{CM14} and, for linear systems, Cleve, Liu and Slofstra~\cite{CLS17}. Slofstra established an embedding theorem for solution groups~\cite{Slo20} and the non-closure of quantum correlations~\cite{Slo19}. Slofstra and Vidick introduced the hyperlinear profile as a quantitative bridge from approximate representations to dimension requirements~\cite{SV18}. Slofstra later obtained a finitely presented group with at least subexponential hyperlinear profile~\cite{Slo18}. Ji, Leung and Vidick construct a fixed finite three-player game of value one for which near-perfect play requires at least $\Omega(\eps^{-c})$ qubits for a fixed $c>0$~\cite{JLV20}. Coladangelo constructs a fixed finite two-player score game for which every $\eps$-optimal strategy has local dimension $2^{\Omega(\eps^{-1/8})}$~\cite{Col20}. These results supply the framework and important growth examples; they do not identify the matched finite exponent in~\eqref{eq:main}.

Mehta, Slofstra and Zhao construct halting-dependent Pauli systems and embed them in finite presentations with polynomial bounds on relation decompositions~\cite[Proposition~4.8]{MSZ23}. Those bounds transfer lower estimates; they do not assert small simultaneous finite-model extensions. The construction here supplies its own upper models. The conjugacy gadgets used in our compiler are those of Fanizza, Kroell, Mehta, Paddock, Rochette, Slofstra and Zhao~\cite[Lemma~5.8 and Theorem~5.5]{FKMPRSZ25}. Their credit is essential: the passage from nested conjugacy to ordinary Boolean contexts is not a new gadget of this paper. We give its matrices and converse calculation because quantitative completeness is needed here.

The state-preserving Boolean correspondence was established in \emph{The price of precision}~\cite[Theorem~4.9]{DM-precision}; its linear-system predecessor and further dimension examples appear in the companion paper~\cite{DM-game}. We use that correspondence as an existing theorem. Section~\ref{sec:game} records the elementary square-root estimate needed for the present local-dimension application and checks the constants' effect on the exponent. The construction partially addresses the rate question of~\cite[Problem~18.3]{DM-precision}, for a different resource: local dimension.

Uncomputable asymptotic data in finite systems have other precedents. Hochman and Meyerovitch characterize the entropies of multidimensional shifts of finite type and realize noncomputable extremal frequencies~\cite{HM10}. Sapir, Birget and Rips relate group isoperimetry and computation~\cite{SBR02}; a Dehn exponent is not a dimension exponent, and no identification between them is used here. Cubitt, Perez-Garcia and Wolf prove undecidability of the spectral gap over families of local Hamiltonians~\cite{CPGW22}. Bausch, Cubitt and Watson obtain uncomputable phase diagrams for a continuous one-parameter family~\cite{BCW21}. These are precedents for asymptotic noncomputability, rather than proofs of~\eqref{eq:main}.

The binary adding machine used below is the classical odometer on a Cantor set; its interpretation through operator algebras is standard~\cite{HSWZ13}. The new point needed here is a finite polynomial presentation and a carry estimate that remain quantitative in a non-flat state. The final information bound uses Holevo's theorem~\cite{Hol73} and the random-access-code principle exemplified by Nayak~\cite{Nay99}. Its branching estimate is proved explicitly.

\subsection{Proof outline}
At precision $4^{-N}$, three compulsory clocks cost $3N-o(N)$ bits of logarithmic dimension. They hold an address, fuel, and time. A finite counter program recognizes the halting set. Reset relations force an ordinary initialized computation on each fixed address cylinder and each sufficiently long fuel sector. Exact tracial models must therefore flag every halting address. Compactness extends the flag-density conclusion uniformly to approximate models, without an effective modulus.

A fourth, conditional clock contributes one further bit at each active address. Its contribution is $\thetaK N-o(N)$. In the upper models, the program is a collection of diagonal labels on the existing clock registers. Its histories require no further tensor factor. A moving register of optional bits realizes the conditional clock in dimension $2^{\theta_N N+O(\log N)}$, where $\theta_N\uparrow\thetaK$ is computable. Source precision $4^{-N}$ becomes game loss of order $16^{-N}$. This accounts for the denominator four in~\eqref{eq:main}.
